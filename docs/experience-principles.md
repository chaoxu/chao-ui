# Experience principles

`chao-ui` governs the conditions under which people perceive, understand, feel, decide, and act. Aesthetic quality arises from the interaction among an artifact, its content, its audience, and its context. A palette or visual style is one parameterization of that interaction.

Scientific editorial is a derived profile. Its dark fields, pale specimens, measurement marks, and institutional typography are contingent choices that produce a particular experience. They are not the source principles of `chao-ui`.

## Using this guide

For a new artifact or a substantial change in visual direction, use the [design brief](#brief-for-any-new-artifact) to record the intended experience and the [review gates](#review-gates) to check the result. Read the research sections relevant to those decisions. The [README task router](../README.md#choose-a-task) identifies the profile guide, implementation, and specimen. For a routine edit to an established artifact, consult the sections affected by the change.

## Provenance and evidence boundary

The public programme for the 2026 SISU-LMU-VDW Summer School, “The Power of Aesthetics,” identifies Helmut Leder’s lecture as “Empirical Aesthetics - History, Concepts, and Studies on ‘What is Beauty?’” and Elvira Barkhatova’s lecture as “Fletz, Bummelzwock, and the Eye of the Beholder.” The programme connects empirical aesthetics with Barkhatova’s work in cognitive linguistics, cognitive neology, lexical beauty, and manipulation analysis.

The wider programme contributes research lines on temporal windows, anthropological universals and cultural specifics, multiscale organization, art in laboratories and galleries, spatial presence and light, wonder, complementarity, motivation and manipulation, explanatory elegance, and visual rhythm. Lecture titles identify questions worth following. Published work supplies the claims adopted here.

The public record does not include the lecture slides. The principles below synthesize the researchers’ published work. They do not reconstruct unpublished lectures or claim that the faculty proposed a joint design system.

## The experience unfolds over time

Leder and colleagues model aesthetic experience as a sequence of overlapping processes with feedback, continuous affective evaluation, context, and social interaction. The sequence provides a useful design model beyond art:

```text
purpose + context + audience expectations
                    ↓
perceptual organization
                    ↓
familiarity + memory integration
                    ↓
classification of content + form
                    ↓
interpretation + cognitive mastering
                    ↓
evaluation, emotion, judgment, and action
                    ↺
       feedback, learning, and social response
```

Fast perceptual and affective processes overlap with slower interpretation. Later understanding can revise an earlier impression. Context begins shaping the experience before the artifact is perceived.

## Cognition has rhythm

Pöppel describes temporal windows at different scales, including elementary processing around 30 to 40 milliseconds and an integration window around three seconds that appears in perception, movement, speech, short-term memory, poetry, music, and visual art. Bao and colleagues likewise treat visual experience as temporal, even when its object appears spatial and static.

Use time as a design material:

- group related changes into perceivable episodes
- preserve the identity of content long enough for orientation and comparison
- establish rhythm through pauses, sequencing, repetition, and controlled change
- avoid simultaneous transitions that compete for the same attention
- let users pause, revisit, and control consequential sequences

These findings do not make three seconds a universal animation duration. They establish that timing, segmentation, and continuity require deliberate testing rather than being treated as implementation residue.

## Context precedes form

Define the episode before selecting a visual treatment:

- purpose and required outcome
- audience, expertise, language, and prior knowledge
- physical, social, cultural, and technical setting
- available attention and expected duration
- stakes, reversibility, and consequences of error
- surrounding discourse and expectations

The same form can produce different responses in a laboratory, museum, dashboard, classroom, public campaign, or private workspace. A reusable profile must state the contexts for which it was designed.

## Complementarity replaces single-cause rules

Bao, von Stosch, Park, and Pöppel propose complementarity as a generative thought pattern for aesthetic appreciation and cognitive appraisal. Their argument rejects explanations that reduce an experience to a single cause.

Review important decisions through interacting pairs:

- universal capacities and cultural learning
- object and context
- content and form
- spatial organization and temporal rhythm
- automatic perception and deliberate interpretation
- cognition and emotion
- individual response and social circulation
- analytic measurement and lived presence

The pairs are not forced oppositions. They reveal variables that constrain and complete each other. A rule that works only by suppressing one side is a profile choice that requires an explicit context, not a general principle.

## Culture and identity shape the frame

Bao and colleagues found that Chinese and Western participants preferred paintings from their own cultural traditions, while both groups showed a cross-cultural preference for landscapes over people in scenes. Their analysis also contrasts object-centered central perspective with context-oriented and shifting viewpoints.

Design for both shared human capacities and learned cultural frames:

- test grouping, symbols, reading direction, spatial viewpoint, terminology, and emotional register with the intended cultures
- treat localization as conceptual adaptation rather than string substitution
- preserve multiple viewpoints when the subject is relational or context-dependent
- avoid presenting one culturally familiar convention as a perceptual universal
- state where evidence supports a broad tendency and where it supports only a studied population

Language is part of the frame. Translation can preserve dictionary meaning while changing connotation, agency, intimacy, temporality, or social distance.

## Perceptual fluency establishes entry

The first encounter should reveal organization quickly. Use hierarchy, grouping, contrast, order, legibility, scale, proximity, and stable alignment to help people locate the artifact’s subject and available actions.

Perceptual fluency concerns the ease of identifying and organizing the presented form. It does not require uniformity, symmetry, visual minimalism, or immediate explanation of every idea. Complexity is acceptable when the structure that contains it remains visible.

For functional interfaces, navigation and primary actions require high perceptual fluency. Editorial and exploratory work may use richer composition, but the route into the work must remain recoverable.

## Structure must cohere across scales

The summer school connects Logothetis’s multiscale study of brain systems with Kadir, Rogala, Bajno, and colleagues’ analysis of visual art through persistent homology. Their art study found that topological structure across scales distinguished sets of abstract paintings that common statistical image properties did not, and related those structures to gaze fixation.

Inspect every artifact at several scales:

- mark, word, icon, or datum
- control, sentence, figure, or local group
- section, panel, page, or screen
- journey, document, workflow, or exhibition
- shared system, organization, and social context

Local polish cannot repair a broken journey. Global consistency cannot excuse illegible details. Repeated relationships should remain recognizable as attention moves between scales.

## Familiarity provides scaffolding

Conventions, prototypes, repeated patterns, and learned tokens help people recognize what kind of artifact they are encountering. Preserve familiar structure wherever novelty carries no meaning.

Use novelty to express a real distinction, invite investigation, or recategorize a concept. Introduce it against enough familiar structure that the audience can form and test an interpretation. A novel control that behaves like no known control spends attention without adding meaning.

## Content and form remain distinct

Identify both layers for every design:

- **Content:** the subject, claim, state, task, evidence, or event being represented
- **Form:** the visual, verbal, spatial, temporal, and interactive means used to represent it

Form can alter how content is classified and felt. It should support the content’s meaning and required action without impersonating evidence. A polished scientific surface cannot confer scientific validity on an unsupported claim.

## Meaning is progressively mastered

People seek coherence by relating present material to knowledge, memory, and context. Progress toward a workable interpretation can itself be rewarding. Design should make meaningful progress possible:

- reveal structure before detail
- provide titles, labels, captions, examples, and contextual cues when they improve interpretation
- expose relationships and consequences rather than presenting isolated decoration
- let users inspect deeper layers without losing their place
- preserve enough ambiguity for inquiry only when the artifact supplies a route toward resolution

Leder, Carbon, and Ripsas found that descriptive titles helped understanding during brief exposure, while elaborative titles became more useful with additional time. Meaning cannot always be compressed into the first glance. Match explanatory depth to the time course of the encounter.

## Fluency and challenge are calibrated separately

Belke, Leder, Strobach, and Carbon found that semantically related titles increased appreciation by facilitating higher-order processing. Their account treats fluency as progress toward recognition, coherence, and interpretation rather than a property of an object alone.

Design different layers at different difficulty levels:

- operations, navigation, safety, and status should be immediately fluent
- subject matter may remain demanding when the work itself is demanding
- editorial form may create curiosity through novelty or controlled ambiguity
- interpretation should become more coherent through engagement

Constant ease can become empty familiarity. Persistent friction becomes failure. Productive challenge creates observable progress.

## Judgment and emotion are separate outcomes

Leder’s model distinguishes aesthetic judgment from aesthetic emotion. People can understand and judge an artifact highly without feeling moved by it. They can also feel moved by something they understand poorly.

Evaluate both outcomes:

- Does the artifact support an informed judgment and successful action?
- Does its affective character fit the subject and context?

Positive emotion is not the universal target. Awe, curiosity, tension, gravity, tenderness, unease, or restraint may fit the work. Functional success remains independently testable.

## Wonder can organize inquiry

Fingerhut and Prinz propose that wonder both evaluates art and motivates cognitive engagement with expansive features. Fingerhut’s later account treats wonder, interest, and being moved as emotions that can structure exploration and shifts in understanding.

Use wonder when the subject supports a genuine expansion of perspective:

- expose a relationship, scale, mechanism, or possibility worth investigating
- provide enough structure for curiosity to become inquiry
- let discovery alter the user’s model of the subject
- preserve the route from emotional attention to evidence and understanding

Spectacle without an interpretive return creates arousal rather than insight. Routine tasks usually benefit from calm fluency instead.

## Expertise changes the episode

Knowledge changes classification, interpretation, emotional distance, and tolerance for difficult material. Leder and colleagues found that art experts responded less extremely to negative imagery and appreciated negative art more than non-experts.

Design for the audience’s actual expertise:

- novices need recognizable categories, terminology support, examples, and visible consequences
- experienced users benefit from denser information, direct manipulation, shortcuts, and fewer repeated explanations
- mixed audiences need progressive disclosure and stable paths between summary and detail

Expert compression must not remove the evidence or context a non-expert needs to make an informed decision.

## Artifacts participate in memory

Vzorin and colleagues found that people’s memory for externally stored information depends on the task and on confidence that the information will remain available. Their work treats digital offloading as purposeful activity rather than automatic cognitive decline.

Design tools as dependable external cognition:

- preserve stable locations, identifiers, history, and retrieval paths
- make saved state and synchronization status visible
- support recognition and reconstruction instead of demanding verbatim recall
- retain gist, provenance, and the link from summaries to underlying evidence
- make export and recovery possible when future access is uncertain

Externalization does not guarantee better memory. The system earns offloading only when later access is reliable and the user understands what was retained.

## Concepts are represented through frames

Barkhatova’s cognitive-neology work treats new words as representations of conceptual structures rather than ornamental labels. New forms align word-formation models with frames, conceptual mappings, metonymy, metaphor, and blending. Her event analysis organizes concepts at superordinate, basic, and subordinate levels and examines which frame slots a culture or language foregrounds.

Represent each important concept, task, or event as a frame. Relevant slots may include:

- actor or agent
- recipient or affected party
- object or subject
- action or change
- goal
- result and post-effects
- cause and conditions
- evidence and source
- time and place
- degree, scale, or confidence
- instrument or control
- uncertainty and alternative interpretation
- counter-agent, recovery, or appeal path

Choose the slots the task requires. Omission is also a framing decision. Never omit the actor, goal, evidence, uncertainty, or consequence when that omission would change a reasonable interpretation.

## Names are conceptual interfaces

Barkhatova’s summer-school lecture title places word form and the beholder together. `Fletz` is an obscure southern-German word recorded for an entrance hall or corridor. `Bummelzwock` has no contemporary DWDS entry. Without lecture materials, its status and use remain unresolved. The contrast still exposes two different design problems:

- **Lexical invention** creates a handle for a new concept and locates it within categories and frames.
- **Entitling** supplies interpretive context for an existing object, image, action, or body of work.

Leder, Carbon, and Ripsas found that title effects depend on the title type and the time available for interpretation. Descriptive titles supported understanding during brief exposure. Elaborative titles became more useful with additional viewing time. Better understanding did not automatically produce greater appreciation.

Treat names, headings, commands, routes, files, variables, statuses, agents, and features as part of the experience:

- identify the category and the distinction the name must carry
- align the name with the concept’s load-bearing frame slots
- reuse familiar words or morphemes when they provide accurate scaffolding
- introduce a novel form only when the concept itself is novel or recategorized
- supply a definition, example, or elaborative cue when the name cannot carry the full meaning
- test pronunciation, connotation, translation, abbreviation, searchability, and confusion with neighboring terms
- separate the human-facing name from a durable identifier when names may evolve
- preserve aliases, redirects, and migration history so renaming does not destroy retrieval

Test classification, understanding, recall, search, and affect separately. A memorable name can misclassify its subject. An accurate name can still be hard to retrieve. A title can guide interpretation without increasing liking.

## Form maps concepts across media

Typography, image, color, motion, sound, copy, and interaction should express the same conceptual frame. A visual metaphor or conceptual blend is useful when people can recover the mapping and when it clarifies a relationship that literal representation would obscure.

Check each transfer:

- What source concept is being borrowed?
- What target concept is being explained?
- Which relationship survives the transfer?
- Which implication might be introduced accidentally?
- Can the audience verify the intended mapping from the artifact itself?

Reuse relationships, not decorative nouns. A topic does not become scientific because it contains grids, coordinates, glass, particles, or equations.

## Intent must survive transmission

Rogala, Bajno, and Wróbel describe artistic appreciation as depending partly on whether an audience can abstract and decode intent through previously learned conceptual structures. Their account also allows new or merged conceptual networks to form during engagement.

State the intended relationship in the artifact rather than relying on the creator’s private explanation:

- encode intent through content, structure, behavior, and context together
- test what viewers infer without being told the intended answer
- distinguish productive reinterpretation from failure to communicate a required fact or action
- supply a conceptual entry point when the audience cannot reasonably possess the needed dictionary
- preserve room for new interpretation in expressive work while keeping functional contracts exact

The surrounding environment is part of transmission. Light, scale, distance, device, sound, motion, and bodily position can change what is noticed and understood. Validate consequential experiences in their real setting, not only in isolated screenshots or laboratory conditions.

## Aesthetic influence preserves autonomy

Barkhatova and Boyarskaya distinguish transparent persuasion from manipulation that bypasses rational judgment through concealment, emotional exploitation, selective framing, or withheld information. Their frame analysis treats the agent, recipient, goal, message, form, perception, post-effects, and counter-agents as explicit parts of the communicative event.

`chao-ui` may direct attention and create emotion. It must preserve the user’s ability to understand, verify, and choose. Reject:

- fearmongering and false urgency
- emotional pressure presented as evidence
- absolute claims that erase relevant uncertainty
- vague authority and unsourced factual claims
- biased labels that pre-judge the subject
- selective statistics, cropped context, or misleading axes
- ambiguous diagrams that support incompatible readings without disclosure
- invented conflicts, forced frustration, guilt, false empathy, or false rapport
- color, composition, motion, or defaults that conceal agency or consequences

State sources, uncertainty, intent, and consequences at the point where they affect a decision. Affect should support attention and comprehension rather than bypass judgment.

## Design the complete time course

Treat the artifact as an episode with distinguishable moments:

| Moment | Design responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| Before contact | set accurate expectations through context, title, entry point, and provenance |
| First glance | expose subject, hierarchy, state, and available action |
| Orientation | establish category, controls, terminology, and boundaries |
| Exploration | reveal relationships, evidence, alternatives, and deeper structure |
| Mastery | let the user form, test, and revise an interpretation |
| Action | make consequences, reversibility, and feedback explicit |
| After-state | preserve result, provenance, recovery path, and next context |
| Social circulation | retain meaning when quoted, shared, embedded, printed, or viewed outside the original setting |

An attractive first frame cannot compensate for a confusing action or an unverifiable after-state.

## Explanatory elegance preserves the load

The programme’s lecture on beauty in scientific work places theory construction inside the aesthetic discussion. For `chao-ui`, elegance means compressing an artifact without discarding the relationships needed to explain, verify, operate, or recover it.

- remove mechanisms, labels, and steps that carry no decision-relevant information
- unify repeated relationships under a stable representation
- keep exceptions and boundary conditions visible where they change action
- prefer a small generative rule over a catalogue of unrelated styling instructions
- measure elegance by explanatory reach and operational clarity, not by visual sparseness

A short explanation that hides the causal chain is merely incomplete. A dense artifact can be elegant when its structure makes many relationships inspectable.

The same principles govern more than posters:

| Dashboards and visualizations | frame completeness, multiscale structure, evidence visibility, honest comparison, controlled attention |
| Documents and mathematical exposition | naming, conceptual hierarchy, progressive mastery, explanatory elegance, source continuity |

| Research systems and workflows | durable state, meaningful compression, inspectable transitions, clear outcomes and post-effects |

| Physical spaces, exhibitions, and events | light, scale, distance, embodiment, social context, and ecological validation |

## Profiles are parameter bundles

A profile records decisions along shared dimensions. It is a reusable answer for a class of contexts, not a universal aesthetic.

| Dimension | Questions |
| --- | --- |
| Context | Where and why will the artifact be encountered? |
| Audience | What knowledge, language, and conventions can be assumed? |
| Perceptual fluency | How quickly must hierarchy and action become clear? |
| Familiarity | Which conventions should remain stable? |
| Novelty | Which distinction deserves an unfamiliar form? |
| Complexity | How much information and relationship density does the task require? |
| Ambiguity | What may remain open, for how long, and how can it be resolved? |
| Meaning path | Which cues support classification and cognitive mastering? |
| Affect | Which emotional register fits the content and stakes? |
| Evidence | How are sources, uncertainty, and consequences exposed? |
| Tempo | What changes across first glance, exploration, action, and return? |
| Accessibility | How does the experience survive different bodies, devices, inputs, and settings? |
| Culture and identity | Which conventions, perspectives, and connotations are learned rather than universal? |
| Scale | Which relationships must remain coherent from local detail through the complete journey? |
| External memory | What can users safely offload, and how will they retrieve and verify it later? |
| Naming | Which category, distinction, connotation, and retrieval path must the name carry? |

The default interface profile favors high perceptual and operational fluency, familiar controls, low ambiguity, restrained affect, visible evidence, and immediate feedback. The scientific-editorial profile combines fluent hierarchy and familiar institutional cues with conceptual novelty, controlled ambiguity, exploratory meaning, and awe or curiosity.

## Review gates

Review the actual artifact against observable outcomes:

- A first-time user can identify the subject and available action.
- The form and interaction preserve the content frame’s load-bearing slots.
- Familiar structure supports rather than erases meaningful novelty.
- Engagement produces increasing coherence instead of accumulating confusion.
- Functional success and affective fit are both tested.
- Copy, image, data, motion, and controls express compatible meanings.
- Sources, uncertainty, agency, consequences, and recovery remain visible.
- The experience works for the intended expertise levels and access needs.
- The artifact retains its meaning outside the original viewport and context.
- Local details, sections, journeys, and system-level behavior express compatible relationships.
- Timing and change create stable episodes without stealing control from the user.
- Stored state remains findable, attributable, exportable, and recoverable.
- Names classify accurately, remain searchable, survive translation, and preserve continuity when changed.

Failure of truthfulness, accessibility, task completion, or informed choice blocks release regardless of aesthetic quality.
